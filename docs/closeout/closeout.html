Limitations we carry, from Phase 7

  • Renovation cost accuracy is not tested and is not claimed (R-14). The two contractor ranges disagree by 36% to 65%, and real costs depend on state, area, labor and material prices. The real number is a local contractor's quote.
  • County size can differ from a listing's by more than 20% when a house has a finished basement or an addition (R-02). The detail panel shows the county size used.
  • Bulk-sale outliers sit among some comparable sales and can move a resale value by 2% to 11% (R-03). Sales above $1,000 per sq ft are flagged "Possible bulk transfer"; the numbers are unchanged, as graded.
  • A plain default look was flagged by our outside reader (R-10); the public site is the brand pass.

Next steps, in order

  • Get the criterion 2 ruling and report against it.
  • Record actual renovation costs on real projects (the evidence tables exist and are empty) so the renovation model can be validated.
  • Re-test resale accuracy on sales after 4 May 2026, once the county republishes, with a protocol fixed in advance.
  • Run the investor blind sort, then add adjustable assumptions (R-05) so an investor can enter their own costs.

Retrospective: what we learned

Each of us wrote separately, before comparing. Both of us wrote, without seeing the other's answer, that the hard part was the data, not the arithmetic; that is the pattern we trust most.

LessonCauseWhat it would have changed
The data was the project, not the maths (both of us)We planned our time around the model (resale value, renovation, ROI) and treated the county data as an input to load once. The engine ran end to end on 20 Oct, weeks early, but Gwinnett's records kept costing us time afterwards: addresses written differently from listings, sales at $0 or at a whole portfolio's price on every house, sizes that sometimes include basements, and a dataset too large for the class server.In Week 3, a plan with real time for data cleanup and real listings run through it first, instead of a loader scheduled as one task.
Agree the hand-off and keep it current (Dhruv)Most of our bugs came from where Daniel's pipeline feeds Dhruv's cost and ROI functions (field names, data types, a missing sale or asking price), not from the logic on either side. Our Phase 4 design wrote those contracts down, but we changed code without changing the contract first.One rule from Week 1: no change to a shared field without updating the contract and telling the other person the same day.
Keep our own record of what we built and fixed (Daniel)We built with heavy help from Claude, and a share of the time and credits went on fixing bugs, some it had introduced itself and some that had been fixed once already. Nothing outside the conversation remembered what had been decided or broken, so each session could repeat an old mistake. This one is on us: we reviewed the code but did not keep the memory.A notes file in the repository from Week 1, read at the start of every session: settled decisions and known traps, so the same fix is never paid for twice.

Method note: what we could not verify

Today's resale values cannot be checked until those houses sell; the back-test is the only accuracy evidence, and it used only pairs whose price rose, so it may flatter flat or falling markets. Estimates ran 3.2% low on average, and lower on the biggest jumps (57% within 15% for gains of 50% or more). Renovation figures have never been compared with a finished project's cost. "Arm's-length" was approximated by price filters. Both outside readers were personal contacts. Demo-account figures use synthetic asking prices.
